2Recurring principles

A · Coincidence detection: one molecule needs two signals

  1. One signal marks which input was active.
  2. A second signal reports that it mattered (US, error, postsynaptic firing).
  3. A single molecule responds strongly only when both arrive together, in the right order.
  4. The change is confined to the marked input.

AnalogyA logical AND gate. It breaks down because the biological detectors are graded and order-sensitive: a weak coincidence at the NMDA receptor gives LTD instead of “no output”, and reversing CS and US gives nothing.

B · The two-stage storage switch: strength first, structure later

  1. Single or weak training modifies existing proteins (short-term).
  2. Repeated, spaced training keeps a kinase active long enough to reach the nucleus.
  3. CREB-dependent transcription produces new proteins.
  4. Synapse number changes, held by a self-sustaining molecule.

C · Bidirectional plasticity: every learning synapse can go both ways

  1. One rule strengthens the synapse.
  2. A different rule weakens the same synapse.
  3. A single variable selects the direction.

4Functional diagrams

The cerebellum as comparator and teacher

Problem solvedA fast movement ends before sensory feedback can correct it, so the correction must be predicted in advance, and the prediction must be kept accurate.

The story: the cerebellum compares what was ordered with what happened, sends its correction ahead of the movement through the deep nuclei, and uses climbing-fiber errors to silence the parallel-fiber inputs that led to the mistake until the error signal falls silent. Wiring is in Kandel ch. 42, Fig. 42-6 and Hirano Fig. 2; this adds what each element contributes.

AnalogyA coach who watches each attempt and adjusts the next. It breaks down because the cerebellum also corrects within a movement through its predictions (the early antagonist burst), and it learns mainly by silencing inputs rather than adding instructions.

5Why questions

  1. Why must the CS precede the US in Aplysia conditioning, and why must the presynaptic spike precede the postsynaptic spike for LTP? What property do the two detectors share that makes order matter?
  2. Why does an NMDA receptor blocker given before training prevent spatial learning but not later recall, while a PKMζ inhibitor given after training erases the memory?
  3. Why does the cerebellum need a low-rate climbing-fiber input when mossy fibers already carry detailed sensory information about the movement?
  4. Why would a system that could only strengthen synapses eventually stop learning, and how do the hippocampus and the cerebellum each solve this with different weakening rules?
  5. Why can a single pulse of serotonin at one branch produce long-term facilitation when five pulses are given at another branch, and what does this say about where the specificity of long-term memory is stored?
