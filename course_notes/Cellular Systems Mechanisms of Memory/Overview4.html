Adaptive myelination — synthesis layer

Connections, comparisons and functional meaning across the five sources. Mechanistic detail lives in the study notes; structural detail lives in the source figures, which are referenced rather than redrawn.

1Overview map

Central question: how does experience change a circuit durably enough to change behaviour — and what does the myelinated infrastructure add that synapses cannot? Sub-question running underneath it: if conduction velocity is adjustable, what is it adjusted for?

2Recurring principles

Five templates that each appear in more than one topic or source. Each is stated once in plain words, then every instance is mapped onto its stages.

P1 · The same messenger, opposite outcomes, decided by its pattern

Template
  1. Activity occurs in the axon or the circuit.
  2. Ca²⁺ rises inside one defined compartment that is listening.
  3. The pattern of the rise — frequency, duration — is what carries the instruction, not its presence.
  4. Opposite structural outcomes follow from the same messenger.
Instance1 · Activity source2 · Listening compartment3 · Pattern read4 · OutcomeKey difference
Nascent myelin sheathAxonal firing, vesicular releaseThe new sheath itselfShort high-frequency transients vs long low-frequency onesElongation vs retractionThe only case where the two patterns give literally opposite structural fates
Astrocyte networkTransmitter spillover, K⁺ releaseAstrocyte cytoplasm, then neighbours via gap junctionsSpread of the rise across cellsSecretion of signals that modulate synapses and blood flowSignal propagates between cells rather than instructing one structure
Neuron-to-glioma synapseFiring of the invaded circuitGlioma cell, via Ca²⁺-permeable AMPARs (under-edited GluA2)Depolarisation, however producedProliferation and invasionThe read-out is growth, and depolarisation alone suffices
Glioma “hub cells”Autonomous, not synapticHub cell, gated by KCa3.1Rhythmic oscillationNetwork-wide growth signallingSelf-generated pattern — the pacemaker logic without a neuron
 Ca²⁺ is the common decision variable across glia, neurons and tumour; what differs is which compartment is listening and what a given pattern is taken to mean.

Takeaway — asking “does activity raise Ca²⁺?” predicts nothing; the mechanism lives in the compartment and the pattern.

P2 · Overproduce, then select

Template
  1. A surplus of candidate structures or cells is produced.
  2. They are tested in place, within a limited window.
  3. Most are withdrawn or die; experience biases which ones.
  4. What survives becomes durable and hard to change afterwards.
Instance1 · Overproduced2 · Window3 · What biases selection4 · What persists
New oligodendrocytesFar more differentiating cells than integrateDays after losing progenitor identityEnrichment raises the integrated fraction, not the number bornA long-lived cell carrying dozens of sheaths
Nascent sheaths of one cellProcesses extended onto many candidate axonsA few hours of sampling per premyelinating cellAxonal activity and vesicular release; the sheath Ca²⁺ signature of P1A stable internode on the selected axon
Existing sheaths during motor learningSheaths already in place, some still growingThe training week itselfLearning increases retraction and halts growing sheathsA transiently bare segment, free for axonal remodelling
Synapses and axon branchesExcess contacts in the developing circuitDevelopment, continuing at lower rate in adultActivity-dependent engulfment by microglia, astrocytes and OPCsThe refined connectivity pattern
 Every level of the myelinated infrastructure is built by making too much and then removing most of it, which is why experience can act without changing production rates at all.

Takeaway — myelin plasticity is subtractive as often as additive; a paradigm that only counts new cells will miss the step experience actually acted on.

Analogy — casting by audition: far more candidates appear than parts exist, and the role goes to whoever fits the scene. Where it breaks down: rejected cells die rather than auditioning elsewhere, and the "scene" (the axon) is itself altered by the audition.

P3 · Activity-regulated courier to a precursor

Template
  1. Neurons fire.
  2. A factor is released or proteolytically shed in an activity-dependent way.
  3. It binds a receptor on a precursor or progenitor-like cell.
  4. That cell proliferates, differentiates, or grows.
Instance2 · Courier & release3 · Receiving cell & receptor4 · OutputHealthy or malignant
BDNF → OPCActivity-regulated neuronal secretionOPC, TrkBRequired for activity-driven proliferation, oligodendrogenesis and myelin changeHealthy — the one mechanistically required component identified
Endothelin → oligodendrocyteActivity-regulated secretion from cerebral vasculatureOligodendrocyte, endothelin B receptorSets how many internodes one cell makesHealthy — the mediator of social-experience effects
BDNF → gliomaSame activity-regulated releaseGlioma cell, TrkB (NTRK2 upregulated by NLGN3)Mitogen; also traffics AMPARs to strengthen neuron-to-glioma synapsesMalignant — same courier, same receptor

 The courier system that lets axonal activity recruit new myelin is the same one that lets an active circuit feed a tumour, down to the identical ligand–receptor pair.

Takeaway — this identity is why therapy aims at the courier (shedding enzyme, receptor) rather than at the cancer cell, and why the specificity problem is unavoidable.

P4 · Control by brake, not throttle

Template
  1. A process is competent to run by default.
  2. A brake holds it back, setting the operating level.
  3. Releasing the brake raises output faster and more reliably than adding drive.
BrakeProcess restrainedWhere it actsEffect of releasing it
M1 muscarinic receptorOPC differentiationOn the OPC itselfDeletion, or an antimuscarinic pro-myelinating drug, increases new myelin and improves recall in aged and fear-conditioned animals
Nogo-AExtent of one cell's myelination; dendritic branching and spine turnoverOligodendrocyte surfaceMore dendritic branching and spine turnover — i.e. the brake couples myelin extent to neuronal structural plasticity
Somatodendritic inhibitory cuesWrapping of the wrong compartmentNeuronal soma and dendritesMyelin is excluded from dendrites — selectivity achieved by prohibition, not attraction
Na⁺ channel inactivation gateThe action potential's own regenerative cycleDomain IV S4 and the III–IV linkerWithout it the spike has no self-terminating step and no refractory period
Juxtaparanodal K⁺ channelsSpiking of the sheathed membraneUnder the myelin, flanking the paranodeExposure by demyelination turns a silent brake into an active block on conduction
 From the spike to the sheath, the system is built as a permitted process under restraint, so the useful experimental and therapeutic handle is almost always the brake.

Takeaway — “enhancing myelination” in practice means disinhibiting differentiation, which is also why the effect cannot be aimed at one circuit.

Analogy — a handbrake rather than an accelerator. Where it breaks down: releasing this brake acts on every OPC in the CNS at once, so there is no steering to go with it.

P5 · Function from confinement

Template
  1. A protein that would do little if spread evenly over the membrane.
  2. Adhesion molecules and scaffolds fence it into a small domain.
  3. The high local density creates a threshold, a trigger point or a clearance site.
  4. When the fence fails, the molecule is still present but the function is gone — or inverted.
MoleculeFence / scaffoldWhat confinement buysFailure mode
Na⁺ channels at the nodeAnkyrin G, NF186, NrCAM; paranodal junctions as the boundaryA low-threshold hot spot that regenerates the spike, with pumping confined to a small patchDemyelination: current leaks, juxtaparanodal K⁺ channels become exposed, conduction slows or blocks
Na⁺ channels at the axon initial segmentScaffolding at the AISThe lowest-threshold point in the cell — the trigger zone that converts graded input to a spike trainShifts in AIS length or position retune excitability
K⁺ channels at the juxtaparanodeCaspr2, under the sheathSuppression of spiking in the sheathed membrane without opposing the nodal spikeExposure converts suppression into conduction failure
Cav2 channels at the presynaptic terminalActive zone organisationCa²⁺ influx exactly where vesicles are dockedTransmitter release uncoupled from the spike
Glutamate transporters on perisynaptic astrocyte processesAstrocyte ensheathment of the cleftClearance fast enough to keep transmission point-to-point and prevent excitotoxicitySpillover and excitotoxic death
 Myelin's electrical advantage and the neuron's excitability both rest on the same trick — putting a channel population in one small place and fencing it there.

Takeaway — this is why node geometry is a plausible plasticity variable at all: change the size of the fence and you change how many channels are inside it.

D1 · Why myelination is for synchrony, not speed

The problem it solves: a downstream detector must compare two signals that left the same neuron but travel unequal distances.

Analogy — a staggered start on a running track, where the lanes are set so everyone reaches the line at once. Where it breaks down: myelin does not shift the start time, it changes the speed of the whole lane, and the same lane also has to carry every later signal.

D2 · Where in the memory timeline each manipulation bites

The problem it solves: reconciling deficits that appear within hours with a structure that takes weeks to build.

5Why-questions

Each requires a connection between sections; none can be answered by recalling a list.

  • Why does blocking new oligodendrocyte formation leave the learning curve intact but destroy remote recall, while interfering with synaptic potentiation does close to the opposite — and what does that pair of results say about what a memory is at one month?
  • Why can adding myelin to an axon improve what a circuit computes even when the added myelin slows that axon's branch down?
  • Why do deficits appear within two hours of training in animals that cannot make new oligodendrocytes, when compact myelin takes one to two weeks to form — and which functions of the lineage must therefore be carrying the early load?
  • Why is the node of Ranvier, rather than the internode, the place where both conduction speed and conduction failure are decided — and why does that make node length an attractive plasticity variable?
  • Why do the manipulations that enhance myelination and the manipulations that accelerate glioma growth converge on the same molecules, and what constraint does that put on any pro-myelinating therapy?
