2Recurring principles

A · Many-to-many mapping

  1. Several sources exist (modulators, causes, disorders)
  2. One source spreads to many targets (divergence)
  3. Many sources converge on one target (convergence)
  4. The observable is ambiguous until the whole pattern is read
InstanceDivergence (one → many)Convergence (many → one)What is observedHow the ambiguity is resolved

TakeawayAt every level the observable is a many-to-many readout, so no source reads cause from a single element; each resolves it by looking at a pattern across elements.

B · Net balance of opposing influences

  1. Two influences push the same target in opposite directions
  2. They interact: add, synergise, or suppress each other
  3. The net balance sets the state
  4. Shift the balance and the outcome changes

C · Fast change versus lasting change

  1. A set point or vulnerability exists
  2. A trigger causes an acute, reversible change
  3. Prolonged or repeated exposure recruits durable change in expression or structure
  4. The new state persists and shapes later responses

TakeawayBoth levels separate what starts a change from what keeps it going; the clinical precipitating–perpetuating split is a structural parallel to short- versus long-term facilitation, not a mechanism the sources link.

Source figureNadim (2014) Fig 1a, left boxNadim (2014) Fig 1a, right boxNadim (2014) Fig 1b

The same synapse can be weaker for one spike, stronger for a burst, and changed for good after pairing; "the synapse got stronger" is ambiguous until you say which of the three you mean.

Analogy: strength is how loud the first note sounds; STP is whether the notes of a fast passage swell or fade; long-term plasticity retunes the instrument. It breaks down because a musician chooses each note, while STP follows automatically from the input rate.

DimensionClassical fast transmissionNeuromodulation
ReleaseFast, synapticOther modes; often diffuse or via the circulation
ReceptorIonotropic, at the synapseMostly GPCRs, but also ionotropic receptors off the synapse (extrasynaptic, presynaptic, axonal)
SignalPhasic, point-to-pointTonic or broad (e.g. low-concentration GABA)

Same molecule, both rolesACh at the neuromuscular junction · synaptic GABAA · AMPA in fast glutamatergic transmissionACh mostly in the CNS · extrasynaptic GABAA · presynaptic AMPA actions

Modulation is defined by what a signal does to the circuit, not by which molecule or receptor type carries it.

One switch or many dials

Field"One switch" view"Many dials" viewHow the source reconciles them
Neuromodulation mechGlobal view: one or a few modulators set a brain state; presence ≈ behavioural stateCellular view: many modulators act on every neuron, always, in balanceNot contradictory data; a problem of bridging levels of analysis
Classification clinCategorical: qualitative difference, ill or not illDimensional: a continuum with an agreed threshold, like hypertensionCategorical is used for clinical convenience, though natural borders are unlikely
Research grouping funcDSM groups: well vs ill by symptom countRDoC: constructs from normal to abnormalRDoC is a research framework and does not replace DSM/ICD

In both the cell and the clinic, the "switch" view is the convenient summary and the "dials" view is closer to the data; each source keeps the switch for practical use while doubting it describes nature.

Analogy: a light switch versus a dimmer. It breaks down because the real system has many dials acting on one another, not a single dimmer.

4Functional diagrams

Sensitisation in Aplysia

ProblemHow does a reflex that normally habituates become stronger after a harmful event, and stay stronger with training?

Between the two, prolonged 5-HT also recruits presynaptic PKC and postsynaptic Ca²⁺ pathways that insert AMPA-like receptors (intermediate-term facilitation). Steps: Notes §6.9.

Specificity from local signalling complexes

ProblemA modulator reaches the whole cell and second messengers act widely, yet only one channel type in one compartment changes. How?

Pattern from Nadim (2014): dopamine (via PKC) inhibits T-type Ca²⁺ channels at the axon initial segment, but not Na⁺ channels there or T-type channels in soma and dendrites; local complexes are the explanation the source proposes. Notes §6.3.

5Why questions

  1. A modulator can weaken a synapse's response to a single spike yet increase its effect during a burst. Why is this possible, and why does "synaptic strength went down" not predict what the network will do?
  2. Sadness in the clinic and dopamine in a rhythmic circuit pose the same interpretive problem. What is it, and why do differential classification and circuit mapping both solve it by reading a pattern rather than a single element?
  3. Why does PKA activation alone produce LTP regardless of spike timing, and what does that imply for calling the STDP timing rule "the" learning rule?
  4. Why is someone who just misses DSM criteria informative for RDoC but invisible to a categorical study, and how does the hypertension example show that the threshold is a choice rather than a discovery?
  5. The Aplysia reflex separates a fast PKA effect from a slow CREB-dependent one. In what sense does this mirror the split between precipitating and perpetuating factors, and where does the parallel stop holding?
